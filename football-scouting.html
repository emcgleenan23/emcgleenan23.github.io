Football Analytics · Open Data

Can open data spot the next €30 million player before the market does?

Using only free FBref and Transfermarkt data, I built a screen for undervalued young players across Europe's top leagues, then back-tested whether its picks actually paid off. They did. A model trained on 70,000 player-seasons then showed why.

Eamon McGleenan · Predictive Sports Analytics

Every summer, clubs spend billions chasing the same insight: which cheap young player is about to become expensive. The received wisdom is that you need scouts, contacts and proprietary tracking data. This project asked a smaller question — how far can you get with data anyone can download?

The answer: further than you'd think, with important caveats. A transparent screen built from open data ranked its top-20 players at a 55% "hit" rate against a 33% base — and a learned model across fourteen seasons pushed that to a 3× lift, while quietly confirming what football economists have argued for years about what actually drives a player's price.

The setup: open data, ten leagues, one question

The pool is the ten strongest leagues by Opta's Power Rankings — the traditional Big Five plus the Championship, Primeira Liga, Eredivisie, and the Brazilian and Argentine top flights. Performance data comes from FBref (StatsBomb-powered per-90 metrics for roughly 6,700 players); market values, ages, positions and transfer histories from a Transfermarkt-derived dataset.

The brief: find players who are young, cheap, playing meaningful-but-not-nailed-on minutes, and whose numbers suggest more than their price tag — including players who look mis-labelled by position. Every ingredient is grounded in the scouting and player-valuation literature: role-based profiling (Bergkamp et al., 2022), and the econometric determinants of transfer value (Poli, Besson & Ravenel, 2022; Franceschi et al., 2023).

The screen: four signals, one score

Each player gets four scores, always compared within their position so a full-back is never measured against a striker:

  • Performance — attacking output and creation per 90 (shots, key passes, shot- and goal-creating actions).
  • Intensity — pressing, tackling, carrying and recovery volume: an open-data proxy for "can handle a high-tempo game," since real GPS data is proprietary.
  • Misprofiling — a model that flags players whose statistical fingerprint matches a different role than the one they are listed in.
  • Underused — strong per-90 output on a low share of team minutes: the classic under-priced profile.

Does it actually work?

A screen is only worth anything if its picks come true. So I ran a strict point-in-time back-test: freeze everything we'd have known in June 2025, rank the players, then check what happened over the following year. A "hit" is a player who was either sold for a real fee (≥€10m) or whose market value rose by at least 50%.

Crucially, the names hold up. The screen's high-scoring calls that paid off include real breakouts and real sales:

Going deeper: what drives a value rise?

The screen uses hand-picked weights. To ask the data directly, I built a second model — a gradient-boosting classifier trained on ~70,000 player-seasons from 2012–2025, predicting whether a player's value would jump ≥50% the following year, using only what was known at the time. Tested out-of-time on the most recent seasons, it scored a ROC-AUC of 0.81, with its top-decile picks rising at 44% against a 14% base.

That first bar is the whole story of football's transfer market in one image. Age is destiny. Value level comes next — cheaper players have more room to grow in percentage terms — then momentum, minutes and goals. It's a striking, if slightly deflating, confirmation of the economics literature: youth is the single biggest lever on a player's price trajectory.

The honest limitations

This is a real edge, not a crystal ball, and it's worth being precise about why:

  • Age does much of the work. Because "rise" is measured in percentages, young players have a mechanical advantage. The genuinely useful scouting question — which players rise beyond what their age predicts — is the harder one, and needs richer inputs.
  • The multi-season model uses basic stats only. Goals, assists and minutes are in the historical data; xG, progression and defensive volume are not. Whether advanced metrics predict value on top of age is the natural next study, and needs a multi-season FBref pull.
  • Market value is a crowd estimate, not ground truth, and it has momentum — so some of the model's skill is simply "the market keeps re-rating players it is already re-rating."
  • Coverage gaps. The Championship and Argentina are missing from the value dataset, and name-matching across sources resolves about 77% of players.

Ones to watch for 2026/27

So who does it like now? Running the screen over the current season and filtering for the young-and-still-cheap profile, three names stand out — chosen for a spread of position, league and risk. These are the model’s calls, not certainties: think of them as where to point a scout’s eye first.

Right-back · 18 · Elche (La Liga) €12m

Héctor Fort

The confident pick

The screen’s number one, and the profile is textbook. A Barcelona-schooled attacking full-back posting an elite output score while playing only a fraction of his side’s minutes — the classic “producing above his role” signature. Full-backs who create at 18 re-rate faster than almost any profile in football, and €12m leaves plenty of headroom.

Attacking mid · 19 · Ajax (Eredivisie) €5m

Rayane Bounida

The value play

Priced like a prospect, performing like a producer. One of the most-talked-about talents in the Ajax academy, with a profile that spans creator and second-striker roles — the positional flexibility that tends to lift value. Ajax’s well-worn pathway to big sales makes the “pop” mechanism concrete. The cheapest of the three and the highest ceiling.

Winger · 18 · Cruzeiro (Brasileirão) €10m

Keny Arroyo

The high-upside flyer

The market-inefficiency bet. An 18-year-old winger thriving in Brazil is textbook arbitrage — South American wide players routinely see their value multiply on the move to Europe. Higher variance than the other two, but he sits on the cheap side of a transfer market that systematically under-prices talent before it crosses the Atlantic.

Ranked by the open-data screen, which the back-test showed carries a real edge (1.66× at the top) — not a guarantee. Profiles are built from output plus the screen’s signal scores; richer radars follow once advanced 2025/26 data is added.

The takeaway. Free data plus transparent methods gets you a screen that beats the base rate meaningfully and a determinants model that is honest about its own limits. It won't replace a scouting department — but as a first filter over 6,700 players, it points the eye at the right names, and it can tell you why.

Methods, in brief

Performance data: FBref advanced per-90 stats, 2024/25, nine of the ten target leagues, collected via a browser session. Value, position and age: a Transfermarkt-derived Kaggle dataset (davidcariboo/player-scores). Players are resolved across sources by name plus season-accurate club (~77% matched). The screen standardises every metric within positional peer groups. The determinants model is a HistGradientBoostingClassifier; identity and outcome windows are strictly separated in time to prevent leakage. All figures are generated directly from the underlying data.
